# The Fourth Kind of Country: Argentina's Economic Paradox

*By Joe Ewbank*

In December 2001, thousands of Argentines took to the streets as the country descended into one of its worst economic crises. Outside the Ministry of Economy, one protester held a handwritten sign demanding: "Ladrones, devuelvan los dólares": "Thieves, give us back our dollars." (1)

The anger reflected something deeper than an economic downturn. For many Argentines, the crisis represented a profound betrayal of trust in the institutions meant to protect their savings.

Decades earlier, Nobel Prize-winning economist Simon Kuznets was said to have divided the world into four kinds of countries: developed, underdeveloped, Japan and Argentina (2). While Japan had defied expectations through its remarkable economic rise, Argentina had followed the opposite trajectory.

Yet Argentina's history had once looked remarkably different. At the beginning of the twentieth century, it was among the world's wealthiest economies. Its agricultural exports, growing cities and expanding industries attracted millions of European immigrants, particularly from Spain and Italy, who came in search of opportunity.

How did one of the world's wealthiest countries become trapped in a cycle of economic instability, and why has escaping it proved so difficult?

## "Riche comme un Argentin"

In the early twentieth century, the French expression "riche comme un Argentin" ("rich as an Argentine") was used to describe someone of extraordinary wealth (4). *See a photograph of [Buenos Aires in 1900](https://todo-argentina.net/historia-argentina/generarion_80/roca2/anio-1900.php?idpagina=1179) (3).*

At the time, Argentina enjoyed living standards comparable to some of Europe's leading economies. In 1913, its GDP per capita exceeded that of France, Italy and Japan, according to historical estimates from the Maddison Project Database (5).

![GDP per capita, 1860–1929](output/graph1_gdp_1860_1929.png)

Much of this prosperity came from Argentina's position in an increasingly globalised economy. Vast agricultural land, growing European demand for wheat and beef, foreign investment and immigration helped transform the country into a major exporter. *See a photograph of the [grain elevators at Puerto Madero, Buenos Aires, 1910](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Puerto_Madero_Elevadores_de_Granos_%28Olds%29.jpg) (6).*

Yet this prosperity proved difficult to sustain relative to the world's leading economies.

The Great Depression of the 1930s disrupted international trade, exposing the vulnerabilities of Argentina's export-dependent economy. Although the country continued to develop, its economic performance increasingly lagged behind countries it had once rivalled.

The following decades brought considerable political upheaval, including six military coups between 1930 and 1976, alongside repeated shifts in economic policy (7).

**Graph 2: GDP per capita as a percentage of the UK's, 1930–2000 (8)**

![Argentina's GDP per capita as a percentage of the UK's, 1930–2000](output/graph2_gdp_vs_uk_1930_2000.png)

Argentina's relative economic decline is illustrated in Graph 2, which compares its GDP per capita with Britain's between 1930 and 2000. Although the two economies experienced different economic conditions, the comparison offers a useful benchmark: Argentina's GDP per capita fell from approximately 75% of Britain's in 1930 to around 40% by 2000.

The shaded periods highlight three broad policy approaches: import substitution, financial liberalisation and market-oriented reforms. These categories simplify a much more complex economic history and should not be interpreted as evidence that any single policy caused Argentina's relative decline.

In the 1990s, President Carlos Menem introduced sweeping market reforms and pegged the peso to the US dollar, initially bringing inflation under control. Yet the arrangement became increasingly difficult to sustain, contributing to the economic collapse of 2001 (9).

Decades of political instability, financial crises and changing economic policies have raised a deeper question: how can businesses and households make long-term financial commitments when the economic environment is so unpredictable?

## The Missing Investment

One indication of Argentina's difficulties in attracting sustained investment can be found in foreign direct investment (FDI), which measures cross-border investment in businesses and productive assets.

My analysis of World Bank data shows that between 2000 and 2025, net FDI inflows averaged just 2% of Argentina's GDP, compared with 3.2% in Brazil, 2.6% in Mexico and almost 6% in neighbouring Chile. Argentina recorded the lowest inflows relative to GDP among these four economies in 19 of the 26 years examined (10).

**Graph 3: Net FDI inflows (% of GDP), 2000–2025**

![Net foreign direct investment inflows, 2000–2025](output/graph3_fdi_2000_2025.png)

Despite its considerable natural resources and educated workforce, Argentina has struggled to attract comparable levels of foreign investment. While FDI is influenced by many factors, recurring economic crises and policy uncertainty may help explain investors' reluctance to commit capital over the long term.

## Inflation and the erosion of confidence

Argentina's monetary instability has persisted into the twenty-first century. While neighbouring Brazil, Chile and Uruguay have generally maintained much lower inflation, Argentina's annual inflation accelerated dramatically, reaching almost 220% in 2024.

*Note: Gaps in Argentina's inflation series coincide with a period of controversy over the reliability of official statistics. In 2013, the IMF censured Argentina over the quality of its inflation and GDP data, lifting the censure in 2016 (11).*

Such instability has consequences beyond the rising cost of everyday goods. When a currency repeatedly loses purchasing power, households have strong incentives to seek alternative ways of preserving their savings.

For many Argentines, that alternative has long been the US dollar. Argentina is estimated to hold the second largest stock of US currency outside the United States (12).

The expression *"dólares debajo del colchón"* (dollars under the mattress) captures this relationship with money. While the phrase is often metaphorical elsewhere, an Argentine friend explained to me that it can be remarkably literal. "It's very common for households", she explained, "to keep physical US dollar banknotes hidden at home rather than depositing them in banks."

## Dollars under the mattress: The legacy of the *corralito*

As Argentina's financial crisis intensified in December 2001, the government introduced emergency restrictions on bank withdrawals and transfers, known as the *corralito*, literally "the little cage". Intended to prevent the banking system from collapsing, the restrictions left many Argentines unable to access their savings freely. The subsequent conversion of many dollar-denominated deposits into pesos in 2002 further undermined confidence (13).

To explore whether this distrust persists today, I conducted a small survey of Argentine contacts about their memories of the crisis and attitudes towards banks.

Asked what they remembered about 2001, one respondent answered simply: "Devastation, sadness, outrage, anger." Another described it as "the worst period of my life", recalling "total uncertainty, insecurity and fear".

The consequences were deeply personal. "My income fell dramatically, and I couldn't access my money in the bank," explained one respondent. Another recalled their family's business closing, while others described cutting household spending and abandoning future plans.

More than two decades later, distrust remains evident among those surveyed. Asked whether they trust Argentine banks, one respondent replied: "No! Another *corralito* could happen." All respondents who answered a question about keeping physical dollars at home described it as common practice.

Opinions differed, however, on whether younger Argentines share this scepticism. Some believed distrust had been passed down through generations, while others suggested younger people were more comfortable with alternative investments. As one respondent put it, young people may be more willing to take financial risks, "but not in banks".

Although this small, informal survey cannot represent Argentina as a whole, the responses suggest that rebuilding financial trust may take considerably longer than stabilising the economy.

The fragility of financial trust is not unique to Argentina. In November 2026, the collapse of Paraguay's Ueno Bank temporarily left around three million customers unable to access their accounts (14), prompting protests and comparisons with Argentina's *corralito* of 2001. Although the circumstances differ, the crisis offers a timely reminder of how quickly confidence in financial institutions can be shaken.

## Milei's experiment: Can trust be rebuilt?

Since taking office in December 2023, President Javier Milei has pursued an ambitious programme of economic liberalisation, fiscal austerity and deregulation (15). A self-described libertarian, Milei argues that excessive state intervention and government spending lie at the heart of Argentina's recurring economic problems.

His administration has reduced fiscal deficits, eased currency restrictions and overseen a sharp decline in inflation (16). Supporters see these developments as signs of greater stability, while critics question the social costs of austerity. Yet a recent dispute over the government's decision to delay updating its inflation statistics has raised fresh concerns about institutional independence, a particularly sensitive issue given Argentina's history of unreliable economic data (17).

The real test of Milei's reforms may be whether households and investors can be convinced that this stability will last. Perhaps the clearest sign of lasting progress will be the day when Argentines feel comfortable taking their dollars out from under the mattress.
